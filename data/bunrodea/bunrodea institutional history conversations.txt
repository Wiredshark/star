			`A freight-office museum preserves battered cargo seals beside generations of shipping ledgers. The accompanying text describes how Megasa crews came to handle much of the realm's repetitive hauling: food, industrial feedstock, replacement machinery, and other cargo whose importance is easiest to notice when it stops moving. Early ledgers are local and inconsistent; later records use common manifests, liability marks, and destination codes.`
			`\tOne panel is unusually frank about status. Megasa remain the lowest social rank recognized in the old hierarchy, yet the archive notes that their freight networks became too important for aristocratic households and city administrations to treat as purely private servants. Standard contracts and cargo records grew in part because the rest of the realm depended on their work.`

			`Beside a Royal Guard post, an exhibit shows old identification sketches, challenge signals, patrol maps, and damaged pieces of defensive equipment. A translated caption says that the Guard's earliest surviving records often describe unfamiliar ships simply as threats or intruders. Later manuals distinguish trespass, uncertain contact, armed approach, active attack, and diplomatic arrival, with different responses attached to each category.`
			`\tThe final display remarks that the categories did not make the Royal Guard less zealous. They made its zeal more procedural: commanders could compare incidents, justify escalation, and punish guards who ignored an established challenge or warning step. Several revisions are annotated after particularly embarrassing misunderstandings.`

			`A technical archive displays old fuel couplings, cargo gauges, docking fittings, and repair tags arranged from oldest to newest. The translation notes that Bunrodea ports once used a patchwork of local interfaces and workshop conventions. As freight and court administration tied more cities together, port authorities gradually published common dimensions, pressure markings, inspection symbols, and maintenance records.`
			`\tThe exhibit treats compatibility as an institutional achievement rather than a single invention. Workshops could still build differently, but shared measures allowed a vessel serviced in one city to refuel, unload, or obtain basic repairs in another without every transaction becoming a negotiation. A row of modern fittings at the end of the display is deliberately mundane.`
